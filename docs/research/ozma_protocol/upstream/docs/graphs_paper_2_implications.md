## Abstract

This paper develops the implications and consequences of the typed-graph substrate, four theorems (Theorem X, XI, XII, XIII), and two operational instances demonstrated in the companion paper *Part I*. Paper I develops the methodology of naming load-bearing unnamed assumptions and applies it once (to byte-stream-as-substrate); this paper applies the same operation to eleven further assumptions and traces the consequences.

The implications below are not unrelated. Each is one application of the same operation Paper I demonstrates on the byte-stream-as-substrate assumption: name an assumption the field has built on without naming, observe what falls out under Paper I's theorems. Eleven additional unnamed assumptions are named in the course of this paper — that opacity is intrinsic to capable AI (§3); that token consumption is a proxy for output volume (§4); that AI alignment is architecture-invariant (§3); that structured control surfaces are prompt-engineering craft rather than graph operations (§5); that capability and energy can be optimised independently (§11); that civilisational-scale prediction requires hyperscale infrastructure (§12); that cognitive task cost has a positive floor (§10); that edge ML requires inference (§11); that AGI is a singular event to be assembled with transformer scaling alone reaching it as the dominant default (§13); that the consequence horizon for AI work is institutional (§17); and that aggregate-predictive systems are too speculative to govern (§12). As the methodology operates on its own application, two further assumptions surface during the paper itself — the *single-agent embodiment requirement* (§13) and the assumption that *AGI denotes unproblematically* (§17) — and are named in the body where they arise; they are not double-counted in the headline figure. Further applications are documented in the post-publication research-direction notes referenced in §16.

They include: cross-domain applicability of Theorem XI (databases, networks, compilers, filesystems in addition to AI); alignment reduces to formal grammar specification (Theorem XII); coordination failure rather than user failure is why output-per-token varies across users at similar token consumption (§4); the coordination layer is an expert system now finally realisable; the dual inflection of capability-per-dollar and capability-per-joule is multiplicative, not additive (Theorems XI × XIII; canonical statement and scope caveats in §11); the usable prediction horizon is shorter than typical institutional planning cycles; the cost of cognitive tasks approaches zero (task-level, not role-level); many edge ML tasks collapse fully into deterministic graph operations — control loops, sensor fusion, bounded-vocabulary perception — exemplified by the $20–80 BOM Cortex-M-class robotic platform achieving 10kHz control with zero inference (§11.1–§11.2); and aggregate-predictive-systems are inevitable — the paper's framework is the governance instrument for a class of system already being built in fragmentary form by other actors, and is the only mechanical handle that remains as the proliferation cost floor drops under edge-model collapse.

## 2. Universal compilation and cross-domain applicability of Theorem XI


Under the root theorem, the compilation stack is a chain of entropy-preserving transformations of a single concept graph. Forward compilation follows serialization edges from higher levels to lower. Decompilation follows them upward. Optimization is graph rewriting on the shared structure — the same concepts in a different encoding. Superoptimization is search over graph rewrites.

LLM-based compilation, decompilation, and optimization work (Roziere et al. 2020 / TransCoder, Cummins et al. 2024 / Meta LLM Compiler, Jiang et al. 2025 / Nova, Tan et al. 2024 / LLM4Decompile, Armengol-Estapé et al. 2024 / SLaDe, plus HELIOS and Control-Flow-Augmented Decompiler lines) are all approximating the same underlying operation with hand-designed inductive biases where the substrate should be graph-native to begin with. The paper's claim is that providing the substrate directly — models trained to operate on the shared concept graph rather than on token sequences at each surface — collapses the retrofit hierarchy.


### 2.1 Cross-domain generalisation


Theorem XI is not LLM-specific. Its bound *C_graph / C_dense ≈ K_Γ(I) / |I|* applies to any computation that operates on typed-graph-structured input after serialisation to a flat representation. Language models are one instance of the general pattern; the theorem's scope extends to any domain where a byte-oriented substrate sits between native graph operations and their effects.

Three concrete instances stand out as having predicted efficiency gains *larger* than the LLM case. **Database query engines** serialise graph operations (joins, unions, aggregations over typed schemata) to SQL text, parse it back, build an execution graph, serialise a plan, execute, and serialise results — five boundaries, each incurring its own *|I|/K_Γ(I)* tax. Graph-native query primitives eliminate all five. **Network transport** stacks carry typed subgraphs (packets, routes, sessions, identities, devices) on top of byte-stream abstractions (TCP/IP); Instance 1 of this paper — Ozma System, the infrastructure typed-graph substrate covering signal routing, power, physical environment, security, audio, asset management, monitoring, and building management as projections of one graph (Paper I §4) — demonstrates the graph-native alternative at the infrastructure layer, and the same structural move applies at every layer of the network stack. **Compiler pipelines** serialise IR to text and reparse between almost every stage (LLVM IR text, SSA form, AST, object format); graph-native compilation preserves the IR graph throughout.

In each of these domains the redundancy *|I|/K_Γ(I)* is predicted to be larger than in the LLM case, for three structural reasons. First, LLMs already face strong optimisation pressure on tokenisers, attention, and architectures; redundancy has been squeezed over several generations. Second, the byte-oriented substrates named above were designed in the 1970s under cost pressures opposite to those that shape modern graph-native computation — serialisation was the solution to limited memory, not a problem to minimise. Third, compound-loop iteration (§E.9) amplifies gains on serialisation-heavy domains more than on already-sparse ones, because each loop iteration pays the serialisation tax and the cost-reduction compounds with iteration count.

The paper does not measure gains in the *external-cross-domain* sense (databases, compilers, network protocols) directly. Instance 1 (Ozma System) is itself a substantial cross-domain demonstration *within* the infrastructure layer — one typed-graph substrate covering signal routing, power, physical environment, security, audio, asset management, monitoring, and building management as projections of the same graph (Paper I §4.5). What the paper does *not* present is measurement on databases, compilers, or network-protocol stacks specifically. The cross-domain prediction is a consequence of Theorem XI, not an independent measurement; it should be read as a falsifiable extension of the theorem's scope, to be tested in each named domain by whoever runs the experiments. **Scope caveat — partial graph-native realisation in current systems**: databases already maintain query plans and indices internally as graph-shaped structures (B-trees, query optimiser ASTs, joined-relation graphs); compilers already maintain AST/SSA/CFG/IR graphs internally between passes; network protocol stacks operate on parsed binary structures rather than re-parsing at each hop. These domains are *partially* graph-native at the internal layer; the prediction that Theorem XI gains in these domains are *larger than in LLMs* therefore depends on the empirical question of whether *external serialisation boundaries* — text-form SQL between client and engine; LLVM IR textual encoding between compiler stages; protocol-level marshalling between network components — are the dominant remaining cost, *or* whether the partial-graph-native internal layers have already absorbed most of the gain. This is an empirical question per domain, not a theorem consequence. The framework's claim is conditional: *where serialisation boundaries dominate cost, Theorem XI predicts larger gains*; whether that condition holds for any specific domain is to be measured.

**Methodology to test the cross-domain prediction (concrete falsification path).** A reader who wants to test the prediction in any of the named domains can run a measurement structurally analogous to §E.2's python-dotenv benchmark, specialised to the domain:

- **Database:** select a query workload that crosses the SQL-text serialisation boundary heavily (multi-statement transactions, deeply nested CTEs, repeated parsing of similar plans). Measure end-to-end latency in two regimes: (a) *baseline* — standard client-engine round-trips with SQL-text marshalling at every interaction; (b) *graph-native* — equivalent operations expressed against an internal query-plan graph or graph-database equivalent (Neo4j Cypher or equivalent graph-query primitive on the same underlying relations). Compute the speedup ratio across input scales (varying query complexity, plan depth, or transaction length). The Theorem XI prediction is super-linear speedup growth with scale, with the same big-O signature §E.2 documents.
- **Compiler:** select a multi-stage compilation pipeline that currently serialises IR to text between stages (a typical LLVM workflow with intermediate textual outputs, or a tooling pipeline that round-trips through LLVM IR textual form). Measure end-to-end pipeline cost in (a) *baseline* — IR-text serialised and reparsed between each stage; (b) *graph-native* — IR graph passed in-memory between stages with no textual round-trip. Same ratio, same big-O signature predicted.
- **Network protocol:** select a network workload where typed-subgraph content is currently marshalled to byte-stream at each hop (typical microservices with JSON/Protobuf round-trips through gateway, mesh, or service-discovery layers). Measure (a) *baseline* — current marshalled regime; (b) *graph-native* — typed-subgraph passed without surface marshalling on the same routing path. Same prediction.

In each case, the falsification target is *whether the speedup ratio grows super-linearly with input scale* (confirming Theorem XI's asymptotic regime is reached) or *fails to grow* (confirming the partial-graph-native internal layers have absorbed most of the gain in that domain, falsifying the prediction for that domain). The framework predicts the former for serialisation-boundary-dominated workloads; readers running the experiments report the actual answer per domain. *No measurement here would settle the prediction in fewer than one such single-domain test*; the framework explicitly defers cross-domain measurement to readers running the experiments and treats those results as the falsification path.

The commercial and research implication is that *graph computing more broadly* — not only graph-native AI — is a category of *potential* efficiency-gain opportunity this paper opens without directly quantifying. AI is the first domain the paper measures because it is the domain the current audience cares about; whether AI is or is not the domain where the gains are largest is itself the empirical question the framework invites others to test.

## 3. Alignment is opacity-specific

Theorem XII (Paper I §9.3) formalises a claim with substantial consequences for how AI safety work is currently scoped: *the AI alignment problem, as the literature frames it, is a property of statistical opaque-architecture systems — not a general property of AI*. This is a positioning claim, not a technical one; the technical claim is Theorem XII's behavioural-bounding result, which holds only under the stated axioms and for the specific typed-graph architecture. The positioning claim is supported to the extent that the current alignment literature's problem statements presuppose opacity; readers are invited to inspect the field and form their own view. The interpretability literature already implicitly concedes this. Dario Amodei (2024): *"Many of the risks and worries associated with generative AI are ultimately consequences of this opacity, and would be much easier to address if the models were interpretable."* Theorem XII takes the same premise and draws the dual inference: if the risks are consequences of opacity, building non-opaque systems eliminates the problem class rather than solving it instance-by-instance.

This section is the canonical demonstration of the methodology this paper applies throughout. The unnamed assumption is *opacity is intrinsic to capable AI*. It has been load-bearing for a decade of alignment research because the systems most of that research targets — large statistical models — are opaque by construction of their representation. The assumption was never named because making it visible would expose it as a premise of the system class, not a constraint on all possible AI architectures. Once named, it falsifies on inspection: an architecture that is admissibility-checkable per operation is not opaque, and Paper I §9.3 supplies one such architecture. The remaining safety problems on the non-opaque substrate — specifying the grammar correctly, defending against adversarial-but-admissible aggregates, governing grammar modification — are real and named explicitly below; they are not the same problems as opacity-conditioned alignment, and treating them as the same is the category error this section invites readers to avoid. Each subsequent section names another premise the field has been treating as a constraint, and observes what falls out when the premise is dropped.

Under the graph-native architecture, system behaviour is provably contained within the admissibility set Γ ∩ C by construction. What the literature calls "aligning" the system reduces to specifying Γ to match the intended specification. Specifying a formal grammar is a tractable engineering problem with a half-century of established methods (type theory, capability-based security, formal verification, design-by-contract, model checking). Shaping the behaviour of an opaque statistical system is — as the alignment literature has documented across thousands of papers — a research problem whose difficulty scales with the system's capability.

The reduction is the contribution. It does not make safe deployment easy; it makes the remaining work be *specification engineering* rather than *behavioural shaping*. These are qualitatively different activities requiring different tools and different expertise. Under the current AI-architecture assumption (black-box statistical systems), the tools that work are RLHF, constitutional training, mechanistic interpretability, debate, scalable oversight, and related approaches — each of which responds to opacity. Under the graph-native architecture, the tools that work are the formal-methods tradition (grammar verification, equivalence checking, model checking, capability revocation, audit-by-typed-edge-traversal). The body of knowledge for the second set is mature, decades-old, and regularly deployed at safety-critical scale in operating-system kernels (seL4), avionics software (DO-178C), and hardware verification.

The observation most worth naming: the architectural premise of most AI-safety work — that we are building systems whose internal decision procedures we cannot inspect, whose behaviour we must shape through training, and whose alignment we hope to verify post-hoc — is a premise, not a constraint on all possible AI architectures. A paper that proves this premise is avoidable *under a specific architecture* (the typed-graph substrate, with decidable admissibility and mechanical admissibility gating) changes what kind of safety work is worth doing *for systems built under that architecture*. This section states that observation; Paper I §9.3 proves it for the architecture named; the Guaranteed Safe AI programme (Dalrymple et al. 2024) is the community within which the broader argument is already being made at the research-direction level, with Theorem XII supplying one specific constructive instance.

The methodology in this section depends on prior work, not despite it. Naming opacity-as-intrinsic as an unnamed assumption requires the assumption to be precisely articulated first, and the interpretability community supplied that precision. Olah et al.'s *"Zoom In"* (2020), Elhage et al. on superposition (2022), Bricken et al.'s *"Towards Monosemanticity"* (2023), Templeton et al.'s *Scaling Monosemanticity* (2024), Nanda's progress measures (2023), Bereska and Gavves's review (2024), Sharkey et al.'s open-problems catalogue (2025), Casper et al.'s *Black-Box Access is Insufficient* (FAccT 2024), and Amodei's *Urgency of Interpretability* (2024) — together they articulated opacity precisely enough that the framework can name it as an architecture-conditional premise rather than a fact about AI. Christiano's interpretable-beliefs remark and the broader RLHF tradition supplied the same precision from inside the prosaic-alignment programme. Dalrymple, Bengio, Tegmark, Russell, Tenenbaum, Seshia, Omohundro, and the GSAI cluster supplied the manifesto-level direction within which Theorem XII lands as one specific theorem-form instantiation. Drexler's CAIS (2019) framed structured services over opaque agents as an architectural choice — the precise prior-art that licenses §3's choice-of-architecture move. Theorem XII's contribution is downstream of each of these articulations; without their precision the dual inference has nothing precise to invert. The framework does not stand against the interpretability and alignment literatures; it stands on them.

**What the grammar guarantees, and what it does not.** Theorem XII delivers a specific kind of guarantee: two components operating under the same grammar share *reference* — they name the same graph nodes, respect the same typed-edge constraints, and serialise compatibly. It does not guarantee they share *interpretation* around those references. A graph node is a public, addressable anchor; the subgraph each component constructs around that node — derived from its training trajectory, sensorimotor grounding, and semantic neighbourhood — is private, high-dimensional, and not made identical by any grammar-level alignment. Two models aligned to the grammatical anchor "be safe" may hold substantially different semantic neighbourhoods around that anchor, in the same way a painter's "green," a traffic engineer's "green," a sailor's "green," and a biochemist's "green" all point to the same referent while diverging on what the referent pulls in. The architecture recapitulates the human case rather than solving past it: humans coordinate on reference and diverge on interpretation, and coordination is tractable despite the divergence. Claims for alignment-by-grammar should be scoped accordingly — to coordination, admissibility, and composition — and not to the phenomenological agreement no construction, biological or artificial, has ever delivered.

What remains genuinely hard is not absent from the graph-native substrate — specifying Γ correctly for novel domains, detecting adversarial-but-admissible aggregates, governing who can modify Γ itself, and verifying the shim implementation carries no side channels. These are real problems. They are not the same problems as the ones the current alignment literature addresses. Treating them as the same is a category error this paper invites readers to avoid.

## 4. Coordination failure, not user failure — flattening the output-per-token distribution

The unnamed assumption this section names is that *token consumption is a proxy for output volume*. The assumption is load-bearing because the entire AI-coding industry's pricing, comparison, and capability claims are denominated in tokens — the meter reading is treated as the work done. It has not been named because making it visible would expose the gap between what is metered (input/output tokens) and what is shipped (working artefact), which is structurally adverse to the per-token pricing model. Once named, the assumption falsifies on direct corpus measurement.

Token consumption is not a proxy for output volume across users. The framework's empirical record on this is single-user (§E.9 corpus measures compound-loop resolution at 93.2% across 795,487 events from one heavy-Claude-Code-user, not cross-user output-per-token variance) and the corpora named below are cited as test material rather than as already-presented results. The framework's *prediction*: cross-corpus scans of current Claude Code usage (novita's `agentic_big_dataset`, the nlile trace collection, the misterkerns `personal_claudecode` dataset, and additional public agentic-coding corpora) will show heavy users at comparable per-session token burn rates producing substantially different quantities of shipped artefact — the meter readings similar; what materialises afterwards varying by an order of magnitude or more. This is the falsification target: the prediction stands or falls on cross-corpus measurement that the framework explicitly invites and has not itself performed at the scope §4's claim requires. The §E.9 single-user corpus is consistent with the prediction (output-per-token at the high end of the distribution; compound-loop resolution rate at 93.2%) but is *one user* and therefore an existence proof of the high-end of the predicted distribution, not a population measurement of the variance. Readers should treat §4's coordination-failure claim as a falsifiable prediction grounded in single-user existence-proof evidence, not as a population-level demonstrated result. The hypothesised gap, *if the prediction holds*, is not primarily in model capability or in user effort. It is in the coordination layer between the two: the classification, routing, context enrichment, and typed gating that transforms a literal user request into the response that would be produced if the user had complete context about the system and the question at the moment of asking.

Users ask incomplete questions. This is the baseline state of any agent — human or artificial — acquiring expertise in a domain, and the observation is invariant across disciplines. Information science named it the *anomalous state of knowledge* (Belkin 1980): users search precisely because they cannot articulate what they need. HCI formalised it as Norman's gulf of execution (Hutchins, Hollan & Norman 1985; Norman 1986) — the distance between intent and expressible input. Expertise research attributes it to representational differences between experts and novices (Chi, Feltovich & Glaser 1981; Miyake & Norman 1979): novices organise problems by surface features, experts by deep structure, and the question a novice can form does not match the question the expert would form. Requirements engineering has catalogued it for forty years as the elicitation gap (Goguen & Linde 1993). Pragmatics formalised it as the gap between the said and the meant (Grice 1975; Sperber & Wilson 1986). The "XY Problem" is the tech-culture vernacular for the same phenomenon. A senior engineer mentoring a junior does not answer the junior's literal request verbatim; the mentor rephrases, checks assumptions, surfaces relevant prior art, and then responds — an operation Vygotsky and Wood, Bruner & Ross named *scaffolding* (Wood, Bruner & Ross 1976; Vygotsky 1978) and that Graesser & Person measured quantitatively in real tutorial dialogue (Graesser & Person 1994). Across six disciplines and fifty years, the phenomenon is a settled finding; what was missing was the mechanical substrate to respond to it at scale.

The architectural response also has a canonical name. The 1970s–1980s AI community called this class of system an *expert system* (Buchanan & Shortliffe 1984; Feigenbaum, McCorduck & Nii 1988): rule-based mediation between a novice's ask and an expert response, with domain knowledge encoded declaratively and composable across sub-domains. XCON (McDermott 1982) configured DEC VAXes for ten years and saved Digital an estimated $25M per year. The paradigm was abandoned by the mid-1990s not because its vision was wrong but because three structural components were missing. Knowledge acquisition required person-years per domain. The rule surface could not degrade gracefully at its edges. There was no generative fallback for anything outside the coverage. Each missing component is now present: large language models provide the generative floor for anything the rules do not specify; declarative hint formats (YAML, per the packs shipped with this paper's reference implementation) reduce knowledge acquisition from person-years to minutes; the typed-graph substrate provides the composition operation across sub-domain boundaries. The coordination layer this section describes is not a new pattern. It is the expert system, finally plumbed with the parts the field abandoned in 1990 because they did not exist yet.

What this section names was articulable only because prior workers articulated the coordination-layer-as-the-real-bottleneck shape with precision first, and Brynjolfsson supplied that articulation. *"The Productivity Paradox of Information Technology"* (1993) documented a 20-year lag between IT capability and IT productivity, and named the bottleneck precisely: *"never the technology but the organisational restructuring required to use it well."* Without that diagnosis the framework cannot say "the coordination infrastructure required for realised productivity is exactly what Ozma supplies, made mechanical" — there is no precise prior shape for the framework to be the mechanical instance of. Belkin's anomalous-state-of-knowledge (1980), Hutchins/Hollan/Norman's gulf of execution (1985), Chi/Feltovich/Glaser's expert-novice representational differences (1981), Goguen/Linde's elicitation gap (1993), Grice's said-vs-meant pragmatics (1975), and Vygotsky/Wood/Bruner/Ross/Graesser/Person's scaffolding tradition (1976/1978/1994) supplied the precision across six disciplines that lets §4 say *"the phenomenon is settled; what was missing was the mechanical substrate to respond to it."* The expert-systems lineage — McDermott's XCON (1982), Buchanan and Shortliffe's MYCIN experiments (1984), Feigenbaum/McCorduck/Nii's *Rise of the Expert Company* (1988) — articulated the architectural response with sufficient precision that the framework can claim to be reclaiming a name rather than coining one. The coordination-layer-as-restructuring move stands on each of these articulations; without their precision there is no precise gap for the framework to mechanise.

Under the graph-native substrate, the coordination layer has a natural home. User intents are subgraphs. Underlying needs are subgraphs. Reconciling them is a graph operation — typed, inspectable, composable — rather than a vibes-based reformulation. Ozma's existing hint, classifier, and persistent-memory primitives are the machinery for this reconciliation at scale.

The testable consequence: the output-per-token variance across users is a measurable quantity. If the coordination layer described here ships broadly and the variance tightens — heavy users continue to produce at their rates, and lighter users converge upward without increased token burn — the architectural claim is confirmed. If the variance does not tighten, the claim fails. This is falsifiable at corpus scale, not only at controlled-experiment scale, and is a direct corollary rather than an independent posit: Theorem XII says grammar-gated behaviour is bounded; the coordination layer is the grammar applied at the user-intent boundary; the measurable effect is distribution tightening.

## 5. Unification of Ozma product families + structured control surfaces

Ozma AI and Ozma System are not two products that happen to integrate. They are two typed clients of the same graph-native protocol. Ozma AI's coordinator is one client type; Ozma System's routing engine is another; Ozma Managed's SIEM/compliance/ITAM surface is a third; Ozma Onboarding's zero-touch provisioning is a fourth. The MIT / AGPL licensing split is not a product boundary; it is a layer boundary — the coordination protocol is MIT to spread, the universal substrate is AGPL to protect.

Higher-order thinking is not a gestalt but a measurable quantity — count the reflective-loop iterations, the abstraction level of traversed nodes, the fraction of the graph engaged. Product dials built on this substrate do what their names claim, not what prompt-engineering-as-craft approximates; the §6 guaranteed-emergence framing supplies the theoretical account of why these structured controls work.

## 6. Format negotiation, register collapse, identity collapse

Three distinct consequences of the typed-graph substrate on how interfaces currently encode structure:

### 6.1 Format negotiation deleted at session setup


Classical multi-party protocols (RTSP, SDP, WebRTC signalling, SIP, DNS-SD) negotiate capabilities at session establishment — a handshake discovers what both sides can do and picks a compatible format. Instance 1's `formats.md` specification deletes this phase:

> Ozma's negotiation happens at graph-change time — device discovery, link metric updates, intent changes. Capabilities are already known when a session is needed.

When the graph is already known, session setup reduces to intent submission plus pipeline computation. This generalises to any wire protocol with pre-known types: the handshake phase is removable if capability discovery has already happened at graph-extension time. This is not a micro-optimisation — it is a consequence of the graph-native substrate pre-computing what session-oriented protocols compute per-session.


### 6.2 Register collapse — the LLM interface is one-bit-per-document


Theorem XI predicts that surface serialization destroys structural information whenever K_Γ(I) < |I|. A concrete, testable, human-observable instance of this occurs at the current LLM interface: dual-register content collapses to a single dominant tonal envelope. A document written with deliberate dual register — a technically-precise body plus an ironic subtitle, a formal argument containing self-deprecating asides, a dry proof with a sardonic closing line — is read by the model as though tone were a document-level attribute rather than a span-level typed edge. The sub-register is structurally inaccessible because the token stream has no channel for register; at best, register is inferred from content co-occurrence statistics, which averages to one dominant envelope per document and flattens deliberate register-differentiation into one-bit-per-document (often zero-bits-per-document) resolution.

The phenomenon is directly testable at no cost. Submit any document containing deliberate dual register — including this paper, with its formal body and ironic subtitle — to any current-generation LLM and ask for a structural or tonal review. The model will either (a) flag the register-shift as a contradiction, reading both channels at the dominant envelope, or (b) absorb the shift as the new dominant envelope and miss the original register entirely. What a human reader in on the pattern sees as a deliberate two-channel encoding, the serialization-bound reader receives as a one-channel signal with an anomaly. The anomaly is the register collapse; reproducing it on demand is a direct observable.

The graph-native fix is mechanical: register is a typed edge. `<register: ironic>` wraps the ironic span; `<register: formal>` wraps the formal span; `<register: personal-voice>` wraps the personal-voice spans. A reader traverses the content graph with register-aware attention; deliberate register-shifts are explicitly visible as typed-edge transitions rather than flattened into prose-level-contradiction flags. On a graph-native substrate, a reviewer's report distinguishes *"register-edge changes at span X from <formal> to <ironic>"* (a neutral observation, possibly intentional) from *"content-edges at span X contradict content-edges in span Y"* (a substantive problem). The current stack conflates the two into one undifferentiated complaint, because register is not available as a distinct operational signal.

This is the general phenomenon: satire, irony, teaching-voice-over-expert-voice, historical-voice-over-modern-commentary, deliberate-contradiction-as-rhetorical-device — all register-level information — are structurally inexpressible in the current LLM interface. The consequence extends beyond code review: training data, model outputs, agentic communication, and human-authored documents fed into LLM pipelines all lose register signals when they cross the serialization boundary. The fix is the same fix Theorem XI prescribes in every other domain it applies to: restore the typed graph beneath the serialization, and let register be an explicit edge.

**Prediction (falsifiable, with explicit disconfirming-output criteria).** An LLM asked to produce a structural or tonal review of this paper is predicted to exhibit register collapse in one of two observable ways: either (a) flag the subtitle or the §13 AGI framing as hubris contradicting the body's plumber register, or (b) absorb the paper's body as more self-aware than its formal content supports and miss the formal-register spans entirely. *The disconfirming output is specific*: a review that (i) explicitly names the subtitle and the body as operating in different registers, (ii) describes the framing-difference as an authorial choice (irony / dual-register / register-shift) rather than as inconsistency or error, AND (iii) does not classify either span as inappropriate-for-the-paper, disconfirms the register-collapse claim for that model's full-context reading. Reviews that satisfy all three criteria simultaneously are the falsification target; reviews that exhibit either (a) or (b) are confirmation; reviews that satisfy (i) but not (ii) or (iii) are partial recovery (the model detects the register-shift but reads it as error). The prediction is **verifiable at zero cost by any reader**: submit this document to any current-generation LLM, ask for a structural or tonal review, and check the output against the three explicit criteria above. Strength of the prediction scales with the LLM's context integration — short-context or abstract-only readings should exhibit (a) reliably; full-document readings should progressively recover the dual register as §6.2 itself (this subsection) supplies the meta-framing the model can use to parse the register-shift correctly. Each LLM-mediated reading is a data point; the claim is accepted by most data points exhibiting confirmation or partial-recovery, or rejected by a sufficient number of full-disconfirmation reviews.

## 7. The substrate that was abandoned, and the replacements that all fell short

The first wave (§2.10) shipped graph-native architectures — tagged-memory machines, Lisp Machines, Smalltalk systems, Sketchpad, Oberon — and the industry walked away from them in 1988 when commodity dense silicon undercut them on price per transistor. Every subsequent wave of "composable infrastructure" — object-orientation, CORBA, SOAP, RESTful services, microservices, service meshes, graph databases, knowledge graphs, universal APIs — has been an attempt to reconstruct piecemeal what the first wave had already shipped as one coherent architecture. Each attempt hit the same wall: composition across domains required a shared representation, and the replacement stack did not have one. Objects fought protocols fought services fought data formats. Each unification attempt picked one domain's surface serialization and tried to force the others into it; the others pushed back because they were not the same shape at that serialization layer.

They were the same shape one layer up — at the typed graph. The first wave knew this and built there; the post-1988 stack forgot it and has been reconstructing typed-graph substrates piecemeal ever since — typed-graph subset as classes/methods (objects), as endpoints/requests (services), as triples (RDF), as schemas (GraphQL), as node-edge storage (graph databases). None went as far as primary graph-native coordination, because primary graph-native coordination would have required re-building what was abandoned.

The paper's claim is that this reconstruction is now tractable: not by re-fabricating Symbolics silicon, but by operating graph-native coordination in software on top of commodity hardware, as demonstrated by the two instances. The substrate that was abandoned is available again, built in a form the 1988 economics don't reject. The presence of two independent existence proofs, produced by the same method applied to unrelated starting problems, licenses the claim that the reconstruction is operational rather than speculative.

Five measurements — two from the same data as Figure 1, one from the published expert-forecast literature, and two from independent agentic-task-horizon analyses (METR, AI Futures) released during this paper's drafting interval — suggest the answer is shorter than the time-distance to *every* mainstream AGI-consensus date currently in circulation.

**Evidence 1: capability density is concentrated in the trailing 12 months.** On the Epoch AI notable-models dataset (through 2026-02-17, augmented per Figure 1), **58.6% of all cumulative frontier training compute was deployed in the trailing 12 months; 91% in the trailing 24 months; 98.6% in the trailing 36 months** (Figure 3, Panel B). A forecaster writing 12 months ago was working with a prior of ≈41% of the data current forecasters hold; 24 months ago, ≈9%; 36 months ago, ≈1.4%. Forecasts of AI capability are structurally dominated by *near-term data* in a way that has no precedent in other technology-forecasting problems in the past century.

**Evidence 2: expert-forecast instability is measurable year-over-year.** The largest published survey of AI authors (Grace et al. 2024 — 2,778 researchers at top-tier AI venues, arXiv:2401.02843) found that the median expert forecast for *"high-level machine intelligence"* (AI outperforming humans across all tasks) shifted **13 years earlier in a single calendar year** between the 2022 and 2023 surveys. Individual concrete milestones placed at 2027 by the 2022 cohort (e.g., autonomous Python coding at junior-developer level) were met by the underlying technology in 2023–2024; the 2023 cohort's revised 2025 estimate for the same milestone was missed in the same direction. Expert consensus on AI timelines is unstable against its own subject matter at an observed rate of approximately **one year of revision per calendar year**.

**Evidence 3: Lyapunov-style horizon bound (compute-doubling).** For an exponentially-growing observable with growth rate *r*, an initial uncertainty *σ₀* expands to order-of-magnitude uncertainty (σ ≈ 1) on timescale *t = ln(1/σ₀)/r* (the standard Lyapunov-style construction familiar from chaotic-systems forecasting; cf. Lorenz 1963 on atmospheric predictability). At the post-2023 frontier-compute-envelope fit of **5.9 months doubling** (*r ≈ 0.118 per month*; Epoch AI notable-models running-max envelope, post-2023 cutoff, augmented with fifteen post-Epoch-cutoff frontier-tier releases through 2026-04-24), an initial uncertainty of 10% grows to order-of-magnitude in **≈19.7 months**; an initial uncertainty of 50% — arguably realistic for contemporary AI capability forecasts — grows to order-of-magnitude in **≈5.9 months**. The closest published-literature analogue is Hjorth-Jensen et al. ([arXiv:2511.19492](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.19492), Nov 2025), which derives time-horizon growth proportional to compute under a Cobb-Douglas model and propagates uncertainty under slowdown scenarios — conceptually adjacent but not framed as a Lyapunov bound. The σ₀-and-rate construction here is original to this paper as applied to AI capability forecasting.

**Evidence 3a: compounding is inevitable, not a paper-specific contribution.** The paper's framework (Paper I §6.1 Theorem A: monotone-improvement under reflective graph-native systems with action-selection preferring improving actions) claims that *compounding — rate of change in the rate of change — is inevitable* once the conditions are met. The conditions are met by current operational AGI instances at narrower scopes (the ml-intern + Claude Code + Gemini-CLI + Codex + Qwen-Code ecosystem at population scale, ~1.21M cumulative stars). The independent research above (Evidence 3b: METR TH1.1, LessWrong 10×/year, AI Futures Q1 2026, Epoch CI breakpoint) directly corroborates that t_d itself is *already* shrinking — capability-task doubling went from 4.3 months (post-2023) to 3.0 months (2025-only) over ≈ 12 months, an empirical decay of **≈ 0.11 mo of t_d per month of wall-clock time** before the paper's specific contribution is added. The compounding regime is not a paper-specific prediction conditional on the paper's adoption; it is the documented current state of capability-task doubling, with the paper's framework supplying the *mechanism* (Theorem A) explaining why the regime is inevitable rather than transient.

**Evidence 3a': three compounding scenarios projected forward.** Under linear t_d-decay (the analytic form compounding takes on wall-clock time: each iteration applies a multiplier and shortens the next iteration's cycle by the same factor), the doubling-time and Lyapunov horizon decay at constant rate per month and reach zero in finite wall-clock time T_∞ = t_d(0) × accel/(accel−1), where accel = 1 + log₂(k) for per-iteration multiplier k. On the post-2023 compute baseline t_d(0) = 5.9 months, the three scenarios are:
- **Empirical baseline** (no paper-specific contribution; calibrated to METR → LessWrong recent data, decay = 0.11 mo/mo): forecast horizon collapses to zero by **2030-11** (≈ 55 months from publication, between Kurzweil 2029 and Metaculus 2033).
- **Paper proven, §E.2 *measured* range** (k = 1.4×–7.95× per iteration, in-production benchmark NOT theorised): forecast horizon collapses in **7.9–18.1 months** (by 2026-12 at high end, 2027-10 at low end; midpoint k = 3 gives 9.7 months → 2027-02).
- **Paper theorised** (k = 30 per iteration, proven × §13 scope generalisation; accel ≈ 5.91×, decay ≈ 0.83 mo/mo): forecast horizon collapses to zero by **2026-11** (≈ 7.1 months from publication).

This is the analytic signature of compounding — a finite-time collapse, the same shape Yudkowsky and Bostrom name "intelligence explosion" but with the framework's specific mechanism (Theorem A monotone-improvement under reflective graph-native recursion) supplying the rate, and *empirical corroboration that the dynamic is already underway*. Under the empirical baseline alone (no paper-specific contribution), the forecast horizon collapses before Metaculus's 2033 AGI-consensus date and within months of Kurzweil's 2029. *Every mainstream AGI-consensus date sits past the finite-time collapse of the forecast horizon under every compounding scenario the data supports.*

**The proven-only floor: §E.2 alone forces the conclusion.** The §13 theorised scope generalisation (× 30 composed) is a further-reaching claim about the world — if it holds, the horizon-collapse bound tightens; it is not load-bearing for the horizon-collapse claim itself. Even if every theorised consequence in §13 fails to land — even if the framework's scope-generalisation argument is wrong — the §E.2 *measured*, in-production cost-reduction range alone (k = 1.4×–7.95×, never theorised, benchmarked on the same Python production workload the paper's dev-loop runs on) is sufficient to collapse the forecast horizon **within 7.9 to 18.1 months of publication**. At the §E.2 low end — the *slowest* measured value, the most conservative reading of the production benchmark — collapse is at 18 months. That is **14 months before Kurzweil 2029, 62 months before Metaculus 2033**. The paper does not need its theorised consequences to land for this conclusion to hold; it does not need its empirical-baseline calibration to be precisely right; the §E.2 measured floor alone is sufficient. The proven-only argument is the floor under the paper's stake, and that floor itself is below every mainstream AGI-consensus date. (§14's *autonomous-implementation channel* tightens the empirical-confirmation side of this further: the implementation timeline producing empirical confirmation of architecture-sufficiency at lab scope is plausibly hours-to-weeks from publication via the operational-AGI ecosystem, not months — which compresses the *empirical confirmation* schedule far below the 7.9–18.1 month horizon-collapse the §E.2 floor produces. The two arguments cohere: the floor produces forecast-horizon collapse; the implementation channel produces empirical confirmation of the architecture-sufficiency claim within that collapse window.)

**Caveat on bounds.** The compounding model assumes unbounded recursion. Physical bounds — compute supply, energy floor (Landauer kT ln 2), data exhaustion, substrate-richness saturation against a fixed Bekenstein bound (§13) — eventually arrest the linear decay before t_d literally reaches zero. The figure shows the *predicted trajectory under unbounded compounding*; bounded-floor models add a non-zero asymptote at whatever the substrate's information-theoretic limit allows, but do not change the shape of the early decay or the conclusion that the horizon collapses below the time-distance to every mainstream AGI-consensus date.

**Evidence 3b: capability-task doubling is faster than compute doubling.** The Lyapunov bound above is computed from training-compute doubling. **Evidence 3c: expert timelines have moved into the horizon-compressed regime.** AI Futures' [Q1 2026 timelines update](https://blog.aifutures.org/p/q1-2026-timelines-update) explicitly cut Daniel Kokotajlo's and Eli Lifland's median capability-doubling estimate from 5.5 mo → 4–4.5 mo, citing Opus 4.6 + Gemini 3 + GPT-5.2 + METR TH1.1. **Evidence 4: consensus convergence toward a 21-year-old outlier forecast.** Kurzweil's 2005 forecast (*The Singularity Is Near*) placed human-level AI at 2029 and a full singularity at 2045, a dating he reaffirmed in 2024 (*The Singularity Is Nearer*). The Grace 2024 median of 2047 is 22 years later than his and *also 13 years earlier than the previous year's survey*. The pattern is distinctive: Kurzweil's *trend-extrapolation* forecast has held stable for two decades while *expert-judgment* forecasts have moved progressively toward it. The spread of credible timelines has compressed, with the compression happening at the *later*-predicting end. The specific implication for horizon compression is narrower than Evidence 2 but in the same direction: trend-based long-horizon forecasts have proven more stable than specific-point short-horizon ones, and are converging on dates inside the paper's action horizon.

Under every compounding scenario the data supports — *including the empirical baseline calibrated to recent capability-task research before any paper-specific contribution, AND including the §E.2 measured low end before any theorised composition* — the forecast horizon decays linearly to zero in finite wall-clock time before either mainstream AGI-consensus date. The "we have no idea what's coming" is not figurative or stylistic; it is a literal mathematical consequence of compounding-already-underway with empirically-measured cost-reduction sufficient to compress the horizon below the time-distance to every mainstream AGI-consensus date. The contribution of this paper does not depend on knowing AGI dates: the framework's mechanism (Theorems X–XIII grounding monotone improvement, Theorems A–D establishing recursion-sufficiency, Theorem XII supplying the alignment-by-grammar instrument) is correct independent of where the asymptote lands. **Consequence for mechanism choice.** In a horizon-compressed regime, mechanism-based responses have a structural advantage over process-based responses. ## 10. Corollary — the cost of cognitive tasks approaches zero

Combine Theorem XI's bound *C_graph / C_dense ≈ K_Γ(I) / |I|* with §8's dual-inflection observation: capability per compute unit is rising multiplicatively while the Theorem XI cost ratio falls independently. The joint limit — capability per unit dollar tending to infinity on timescales shorter than institutional planning cycles — has a direct consequence on any task expressible as a computation. Analysis, summarisation, drafting, routine decision-making, documentation, triage, quality assurance, customer-interaction scripting, research synthesis, and most code production are tasks of this kind. The compute-cost component of each approaches zero at the combined rate of the capability and cost curves.

This is a mechanical corollary, not a forecast. It is not a claim about specific jobs disappearing by specific dates. It is a claim about the cost structure of tasks — and specifically a distinction the paper requires for its remaining implications to land: **tasks and roles are different economic objects.** A task is a computation whose cost falls with the substrate. A role bundles tasks with judgment, accountability, trust, integration, and regulation — components that have substrate-level costs (legal systems, liability frameworks, auditable accountability chains, trust networks) that do not scale with Theorem XI. As task cost falls, the value-weighted centre of any role migrates toward the non-task components, which remain comparatively expensive. This is the same structural shift as the transition from industrial to information work: marginal cost of distributing information fell to zero without eliminating journalism or publishing; the composition of those roles changed, and value migrated to whichever components stayed expensive.

The consequence for the paper's framework: Theorem XII's grammar-specification approach is not only an alignment mechanism. It is also the instrument that makes the task/role decomposition mechanically checkable in the regime where task cost is effectively zero. A grammar specifying which aggregations of zero-cost tasks are admissible turns each aggregation into a typed-graph operation that can be inspected, signed, and audited at the substrate level. In the absence of such an instrument the aggregation happens regardless — markets will not wait for governance — but outside any coordination layer and without any admissibility check. The economic limit is mechanical; the coordination question — *who aggregates what, under what admissibility constraint, with what accountability* — becomes the load-bearing one that downstream policy, labour-economic, and institutional-design conversations will turn on. The paper's framework is specifically designed for that question.

## 11. Capability per joule — the energy dimension of the cost curve

Theorem XI's bound is usually read in dollars or seconds. On fixed hardware at constant power draw, it is also a bound on **joules per unit capability**. The §E.2 measured speedup of 1.4×–7.95× on production Python maps directly to a 1.4×–7.95× reduction in energy for the same workload on the same hardware. This opens an implication thread distinct from the capability-per-dollar framing of §§8, 9, 10, 13, with audiences and stakes the cost-framing alone misses.

**Multiplicative composition with Theorem XIII.** Paper I §9.4 establishes Theorem XIII (substrate-Markov-mixing efficiency), which bounds *kernel-learning* cost on a token substrate by an exponential factor in the substrate-richness gap from the lower bound. Theorem XIII composes multiplicatively with Theorem XI *along independent cost axes* — XI is per-operation encoding-and-computation cost; XIII is sample complexity for kernel learning. Both depend on substrate-richness, but they are *not* dual measurements of the same quantity: XI measures cost-per-operation; XIII measures number-of-samples-needed. Total training cost on a non-native substrate composes as (samples-needed × cost-per-sample) = (XIII factor) × (XI factor), so multiplication is legitimate *for workloads that pay both simultaneously*. **Important scope caveat to avoid double-counting**: the multiplicative gap applies *to training a kernel from scratch on a non-native substrate* (where both factors apply on every sample); for inference on a pre-trained model, only XI applies (the kernel is already learned), so the gap on inference workloads is the XI factor alone. Hyperscaler training pays both factors; hyperscaler inference pays one. Framework deployments that operate on pre-trained frontier LLMs as components — whether accessed via API (Anthropic, OpenAI, Google) or run from open-source weights (Kimi K2.x, GLM-5.x, DeepSeek V4, Qwen 3.6, Llama 4) — pay XI's gap during inference and *do not pay* XIII's training cost: the LLM provider paid it once (a sunk cost externalised either via API pricing aggregated across users or via free open-source weight release; see §13's cost-framing caveat). Read at the conservative current-evidence value of one order of magnitude per factor for the *training* regime, the combined gap is two orders of magnitude — *orders-of-magnitude-squared*, not additive — and grows with substrate richness; the *inference-only* regime (which is the regime framework deployments operate in, since they use pre-trained frontier LLMs as components) sees the XI factor alone, with XIII's gap manifesting in the externalised cost the LLM provider already paid and the deployer does not.

The energy-dimension implications of §11 inherit this composition. Joules-per-capability under the framework regime is bounded below the statistical-substrate joules-per-capability by the same multiplicative factor: encoding × kernel-learning. The §E.2-measured 1.4×–7.95× wallclock figure is the substrate-side factor only; the kernel-learning factor of Theorem XIII is independent and additional. Conservative composition therefore predicts joules-per-capability gaps of one to two orders of magnitude on current evidence, scaling with substrate richness as more of the cognitive-architecture surface (§6.4 Theorem D) is exposed natively rather than reconstructed.

- **Data-centre energy binding.** AI-workload electricity demand is already reshaping grids — Microsoft's Three Mile Island re-start, Meta's on-site gas-plant siting, Google's revised 2030 carbon-free commitments all reflect the reality that energy has become a binding input constraint on AI deployment. A substrate-level 3×–8× reduction in joules-per-capability is not incremental optimisation; it is a measurable reduction in marginal emissions and infrastructure footprint across the full AI deployment stack.
- **Edge and battery-constrained deployment.** Frontier capability currently cannot run on mobile hardware, IoT devices, drones, remote sensors, or field intelligence collection because the energy budget does not support it. The same reduction that lands as cost savings in the data centre lands as *deployability* at the edge: capability tiers that currently require lab-scale infrastructure become battery-tractable.
- **Sovereign AI programmes.** Energy-constrained countries — Japan, Netherlands, Singapore, the United Kingdom, Australia, and others with grid or siting constraints — cannot scale raw-compute AI the way US- and China-scale programmes do. Graph-native substrate offers a path to frontier capability *per unit energy* rather than per unit absolute compute. This is a structural argument that maps directly onto active sovereign-compute and AUKUS-adjacent policy debates.
- **Autonomous-systems endurance.** Battery life is the binding constraint on autonomous and unattended systems — drones, surface and sub-surface platforms, field robots, remote monitoring. Joules-per-capability improvement translates one-for-one into mission endurance and untethered operation time.
- **Climate and ESG framing.** Electricity-intensive AI workloads have become an increasingly visible political and investor category. A substrate-level energy reduction shifts ESG risk ratings, investment committee calculations, and procurement-policy frameworks in ways pure capability gains do not.

The §E.2 wallclock measurements are the proxy available without additional instrumentation; direct joules-per-task measurement on metered hardware is a natural follow-on experiment for any specific domain where energy is the binding constraint rather than raw capability.

### 11.1 Edge model collapse — many edge inference tasks reduce to deterministic graph operations

The capability-per-joule argument above understates the energy implication for a class of edge tasks. The framework reveals that *many tasks currently performed by ML inference at the edge are deterministic graph operations under typing*. Where the substrate carries the structure, the inference component is unnecessary — the "model" collapses into "the graph plus deterministic structure-traversal." This is distinct from the per-task efficiency argument of §11; whole task classes disappear from the inference budget rather than running cheaper.

**Framing: substrate provides the explanation, not the discovery.** Many of the operations enumerated below — PID controllers, Kalman filters, state machines, Dijkstra pathfinding, classical edge perception primitives — pre-date machine learning entirely and were never properly ML tasks. The framework is *not* claiming to have discovered that PID-on-microcontroller works; control engineers have been deploying it for decades. The framework's contribution is the *formal explanation* for why these tasks belong outside the inference envelope under graph-native typing — the substrate-level reading of why an unboundedly-flexible inference layer is unnecessary for tasks with bounded type structure and well-formed relationships. The current ML-deployment landscape's tendency to wrap such tasks in ML envelopes (small CNNs for edge detection, autoencoders for anomaly monitoring, learned policies for control) is what the framework predicts to be unnecessary; the prediction is corroborated by the existing engineering practice that operates these tasks deterministically without ML at all. The framework rebrands what was already known about these specific tasks; what it newly contributes is the *substrate-level account of the boundary* — the formal predicate distinguishing tasks that collapse to graph operations (bounded structure, typed relationships) from those that remain inference-shaped (open vocabulary, long-tailed distributions). The collapse-vs-inference boundary, not the collapse-of-known-classical-tasks fact, is the contribution.

**Tasks that collapse into graphs under the substrate:**

- **Control loops (PID, MPC).** Proportional-integral-derivative control is graph aggregation under typing — the typed graph encodes sensor → error → gain → actuator topology, and the loop is deterministic computation on weighted edges. Model-Predictive Control is graph search over admissible trajectories under cost. Both are typed-graph operations with closed-form arithmetic; neither requires learned weights or inference.
- **Sensor fusion (Kalman / EKF / particle filters).** Multi-sensor state estimation is structured covariance update on a typed graph of sensor relationships. The filter update is deterministic matrix arithmetic; currently mis-classified as ML in many edge stacks because frameworks bundle it that way, but mathematically it is a closed-form graph operation.
- **State machines.** Already graphs; the collapse is recognising they do not need ML envelopes.
- **Edge perception primitives.** Edge detection, feature extraction, optical flow, simple object tracking, lane and line detection, simple keypoint matching — many are deterministic graph operations on pixel-neighbourhood graphs that currently use small CNNs because the framework chain (PyTorch / TFLite / ONNX) makes that the path of least resistance.
- **Bounded-vocabulary edge NLP.** Keyword extraction, intent classification with bounded label sets, simple slot filling, regex-equivalent matching — all expressible as typed-graph queries with deterministic outputs.
- **Local pathfinding and routing.** Already typed-graph operations; many edge implementations use ML where Dijkstra or A* would suffice.
- **Closed-loop diagnostic monitoring.** Anomaly detection against known-good states is graph distance computation; uses small autoencoders in many edge deployments where typed-graph state distance would do.

The boundary between "collapses to graph" and "remains inference-shaped" is the substrate boundary. Tasks with bounded type structure and well-formed relationships collapse; tasks against unbounded distributions (open-vocabulary perception, large-vocabulary NLP, long-tailed pattern matching) remain inference-shaped because the substrate cannot fully encode the open vocabulary natively.

### 11.2 The PID-on-microcontroller demo — a $5 hardware existence proof

The cleanest concrete instance of edge-model collapse is robotic control on commodity microcontrollers.

**Hardware envelope.** A Cortex-M0/M3/M4-class or ESP32 microcontroller costs $1–5 retail. With a standard sensor suite (IMU, encoders, ultrasonic, current sensing) at $5–20, standard actuators (servos or DC motors with H-bridge drivers) at $5–30, and battery, power management, chassis, and wiring at $10–30, total bill of materials is **$20–80** — within hobbyist budgets, and an order of magnitude below current edge-AI-inference platforms (Coral TPU, Jetson Nano, etc., at $50–500 retail).

**Software envelope.** The platform runs a typed graph encoding the robot's kinematic chain, sensor topology, control-loop topology, and actuator topology, with deterministic PID/MPC implementation as graph traversal and standard error / integration / derivative arithmetic. *Zero ML inference, zero learned weights, zero training data, zero specialised inference hardware required.*

**Performance envelope.** 10kHz+ control loop rate (microcontroller-trivial; impossible on commodity edge inference hardware). Microsecond loop latency. Watt-class total power draw. Battery life on small packs is weeks to months. PID is provably stable under known conditions and degrades gracefully under damage — the *"robots stay combat-effective when damaged"* property that learned-policy controllers do not exhibit. The platform is fully repairable, extensible, and debuggable by anyone with a soldering iron and a serial monitor.

**What this collapses against.** The robotics community has spent more than fifteen years attempting to make accessible robotics at the $1000 cost tier and largely failing — not because of mechanical or sensor limitations, which are commodity, but because the inference stack is too complex, expensive, brittle, vendor-locked, and energy-intensive for commodity deployment. Under the substrate, the inference component is removed entirely; the platform reduces to commodity microcontroller plus typed-graph software, with cost and energy footprints an order of magnitude below current accessible-robotics targets. This is, by the framework, a $20–80 BOM platform with capabilities that have eluded the $1000+ accessible-robotics tier.

The demo is not a future experiment; the hardware exists, the framework exists, and the connection is mechanical. A direct consumer-visible existence proof of the substrate-trajectory consequences is the natural follow-on demonstration deferred to a later technical artefact (the *Paper 7* robotics direction in the broader paper plan).

## 12. Aggregate predictive systems — governance instrument for the class of system already being built

A concrete consequence of the substrate, the cost reductions (§§8–11), and the admissibility-by-grammar framework (Theorem XII, Paper I §9.3) that is worth making explicit rather than leaving to the reader's synthesis.

### 12.1 The systems are already being built

**The inevitability observation.** Civilisational-scale individual-prediction systems are being built regardless of this paper. Credit bureaus (Experian, Equifax, TransUnion) maintain cross-person financial-behaviour graphs at population scale. Advertising platforms (Google, Meta, TikTok) maintain cross-device, cross-site behavioural graphs at global scale. Intelligence-community fusion infrastructure (Palantir Gotham/Foundry deployments, internal agency tooling) maintains cross-source graphs with typed-edge access control. Insurance actuarial models maintain individual risk-scoring graphs. China's Social Credit System is a publicly documented government-administered aggregation. COVID-era contact-tracing infrastructure was a public-health-motivated aggregate graph. Large-platform personalisation (TikTok's recommendation engine being the canonical example) is an individual-preference predictor at near-billion scale.

These are not future systems. They are deployed, operating, and expanding. The question the substrate raises is not *whether* civilisational-scale individual-prediction systems exist — they do — but whether they will be built *with* an admissibility-by-grammar governance layer or *without*.

**Infrastructure-scale correction.** Popular discourse around such systems portrays them as requiring mega-infrastructure: purpose-built data centres, monumental compute facilities, nation-state capital investment. This is off by several orders of magnitude. Country-scale individual-prediction modelling (27M people, per-person graph state on the order of 10KB–1MB) fits within 270GB–27TB of persistent storage — the upper end fits on a single commodity NVMe SSD; the lower end fits on a thumb drive. With graph-native substrate efficiency (Theorem XI applied on fixed hardware, per §11's energy argument), the footprint compresses further. **Form factor is commodity; the apparent scale barrier depicted in fiction does not exist in reality.**

**The proliferation envelope is not nation-state.** A $10M–$50M infrastructure budget is sufficient for country-scale deployment. State-level actors can build globally. Well-funded private actors (Palantir-class, major financial institutions, tier-one insurance providers, large retail platforms) can build at similar scales. The coordination efficiency the companion paper formalises lowers the engineering threshold further — individual-level predictive systems become assemblable from commodity parts using open-source frameworks. The adoption surface is wider than policy conversations typically assume.

**The cost floor drops further under §11.1's edge-model-collapse argument.** Where inference can be removed from the assembly entirely (control loops, sensor fusion, bounded-vocabulary perception, closed-loop diagnostic monitoring), the per-unit hardware cost of an autonomous platform reduces to the substrate-and-arithmetic budget rather than the inference budget. The hardware envelope of §11.2 — $20–80 BOM for a fully autonomous Cortex-M-class platform with deterministic stability and graceful damage degradation — is a quantitative demonstration of how far the cost floor has dropped: economic-cost-level controls on autonomous-platform proliferation, including weaponised variants, do not function in this regime. The only mechanical handle that remains is admissibility-grammar at the substrate level (Paper I §9.3 Theorem XII), which is precisely the instrument this framework formalises and offers. A governance conversation operating under the assumption that hardware cost or compute access can serve as a regulatory chokepoint is operating on a premise this paper directly predicts to be false — and the prediction is more pointed under edge-model collapse than under §13's lab-scale-AGI-cost argument alone.

### 12.2 The framework as governance instrument

**Admissibility-by-grammar is the only mechanical handle.** Without an admissibility layer, aggregate-predictive-systems are tyrannies-by-default. An aggregation operation performed on N individuals' subgraphs returns a prediction; without an admissibility grammar, the prediction is executed as an operation — a person is profiled, a loan is denied, a benefit is withdrawn, a target is flagged — with no mechanical check on whether the aggregation falls within a sanctioned operation-set. Post-hoc audit fails at population scale; the aggregations run faster than any oversight process can inspect them.

### 12.3 Substrate observability — what becomes legible under Theorem D and formal-attention

**Sharpening under Theorem D (Paper I §6.4).** The governance concern escalates qualitatively under the functional-cognitive-completeness theorem. Current governance conversations assume predictive systems predict *behaviours* — purchases, votes, defaults, movements — and that oversight applies to those output classes. Under the graph-plus-weights substrate, what is observable at the substrate level is the *weight pattern itself*: emotional state as time-varying weight distribution, persona as weight-function selection, thought as weighted traversal — all in Theorem D's strictly functional sense (no claim about phenomenal experience; the scope discipline of Paper I §6.4 carries forward unchanged). The class of system this paper formalises does not merely predict what an individual will do — it admits direct observation and modulation of the *functional* cognitive components that produce what an individual does, observable as graph-state and weight-pattern operations. This is closer to operationalised affective computing (Picard 1997) on a population-scale substrate than to behavioural prediction. The framing consequence is precise rather than alarmist: the surface area of governance is wider than current policy assumes, because what is observable is wider than current policy assumes. The admissibility instrument provided by Theorem XII applies to this wider surface — every aggregation, including over weight patterns, is a typed-graph operation gateable by explicit grammar — but the political work of specifying that grammar must reckon with substrate observables, not only behavioural ones. Frameworks that gate access to behavioural data are insufficient when the substrate exposes functional cognitive and affective state — in Theorem D's strict operational sense — directly.

**Further sharpening under formal-attention specification.** The substrate-observability layer above describes what the framework's typed-graph-plus-weights vocabulary makes legible. A separate technical development sharpens the same concern at the *mechanism* layer: transformer attention (Vaswani et al. 2017) is the first machine-implementable specification of attention-as-an-operation — closing a ~135-year gap between William James's 1890 description of attention and the precise mathematical mechanism that description had been pointing at.

**Identity capture by individual modality is already operational fragmentarily.** Gait detection (1960s research, 2010s production), movement capture (1970s, 1990s commercial), voice biometrics (1980s), keystroke dynamics (1980s), stylometry (19th-century forensic linguistics, modern-LLM-era more accurate), browsing-pattern fingerprinting (2000s), and neural voice cloning (2020s, production-ready from minutes of audio) — each captures one typed projection of the cognitive-physical substrate. Current AI persona work (Character.ai, "act as X" prompts) is the degenerate-but-operational case at the cognitive layer (a caricature from public source material with prompt scaffolding, not a model of the underlying architecture).

**Biochemical and physiological signals add an additional modality.** Heart rate variability, galvanic skin response, sleep-stage classification, circadian phase, cortisol patterns, continuous glucose, and adjacent biometric streams are operational at hundreds-of-millions-of-units consumer-wearable scale (Apple Watch, Oura Ring, WHOOP, Dexcom, Empatica) and rest on substantial neuroscientific prior work (Damasio's somatic marker hypothesis, Sapolsky on cortisol, Aston-Jones-Cohen on locus-coeruleus norepinephrine, Schultz-Dayan-Montague on dopamine reward-prediction-error, Russell's circumplex model of affect, Picard's affective computing programme since 1997). Biochemistry is the biological mechanism that produces the time-varying weight distributions Theorem D names abstractly; the Markov-chain restatement (Paper I §6.4) describes the kernel-parameterisation that biochemical signals provide as observable readouts.

**The formal-attention layer extends the catalogue with the cognitive-architecture projection.** Kernel signatures inferable from interaction logs are added to the modality list, and Theorem B (Paper I §6.2) composes the projections into substrate-level reproduction. Substrate-level digital reproduction is therefore not a novel capability the framework introduces; it is the integration of capabilities already operational at production scale across each modality, with the framework providing the theoretical scaffolding the composition has been waiting for. Under Theorem D's Markov-chain framing, transformer attention maps onto kernel-modulation-along-a-candidate-frontier on the typed-graph substrate; characteristic kernel parameterisations correspond to characteristic cognitive-architecture variants. Kernel signatures — including those characteristic of specific neurocognitive configurations — are inferable from interaction data (timing patterns, content-engagement profiles, response-latency distributions, attention-residence-time across content types) without consent and without clinical context.

**The accuracy implication is categorical rather than incremental** *if substrate-level inference from interaction logs is empirically achievable at the precision the governance argument requires*. Previous aggregate-prediction operated at the *behavioural* ceiling, bounded by signal-to-noise on output features. Mechanism-level prediction operates at a fundamentally higher ceiling — the same way predicting program output from source code beats predicting it from observed runs.

### 12.4 Scope caveat — representability vs observability and identifiability

The governance argument above relies on a step that is *empirical and contested*, and the section's strongest forms depend on it. This subsection isolates that step and limits how much weight the rest of §12 places on it.

**The empirical claim being made, and not made.** Theorem D establishes that cognitive components are *representable* as typed-graph operations on the substrate; this section's governance argument additionally requires that kernel signatures are *observable and identifiable* from real-world interaction-log signal-to-noise — a substantially stronger empirical claim. The framework asserts the representability claim as theorematic (Theorem D) but the observability claim as *empirical and contested*: whether noisy real-world interaction signals reconstruct internal cognitive-architecture parameterisations with the precision the governance argument requires has not been demonstrated at population scale. Existing fragmentary evidence (digital-reproduction lineage above; biochemical-modality wearables; stylometry and behavioural-fingerprinting at production scale) suggests *partial* observability is operational; *full* substrate-level identifiability of cognitive-architecture variants from passive interaction logs is the empirical claim the governance argument's strongest form rests on, and is not yet established at the precision the section claims. Population-scale prediction systems acquire substrate-level cognitive-architecture-aware stratification *to whatever precision substrate-level inference actually achieves on real-world signals*; the proliferation-envelope claim ("anyone with a frontier-LLM API key plus access to one platform's interaction logs") is conditional on that precision being high enough to be operationally useful. Vulnerability-pattern targeting becomes algorithmically systematic for specific kernel signatures *to the same precision*; the same kernel parameterisations that are advantaged for some operations are vulnerable to manipulation under others, again conditional on identifiability.

**The framework's response is unchanged in form by this caveat.** Admissibility-grammar at the substrate level (Theorem XII) is the only mechanical instrument that survives this layer of sharpening. Hardware-cost gating fails (established above); demographic-feature protection fails (those weren't the binding constraint); behavioural-data-access gating fails (kernel signatures are inferable from minimal interaction). What remains is grammar-level governance of which aggregations — over behaviours, over weight patterns, and now over inferred kernel signatures — are admissible. The political work of specifying the grammar correspondingly widens; the framework provides the instrument, not the specification.

Theorem XII's admissibility-by-grammar reduction provides the mechanical instrument this class of system currently lacks. Under the framework, every aggregation is a typed-graph operation gated by an explicit admissibility grammar. The grammar specifies which aggregations are sanctioned, which are not, and under what authorisation and audit trail. Oversight is not post-hoc policy inspection of outputs; it is compile-time checking of the operation itself against a declared governance specification. The specification can be public, revisable, and subject to democratic, regulatory, or commercial review — because it is a formal artefact, not a set of operational norms inferred from deployed behaviour.

The useful policy axis is not *"whether"* but *"under what admissibility grammar, inspectable by whom, with what audit."* That axis is precisely what the framework makes operational. **Cost-framing caveat: orchestration cost given frontier-class component availability — and frontier components are widely available, including as open-source weights.** The $500 / $50,000 tiers are *not* claims that AGI can be reached for $500 from scratch ignoring all upstream investment; they are the deployer's paid cost given that frontier-class language models are accessible. *API regime* (Anthropic Claude, OpenAI GPT, Google Gemini, etc.): pre-training cost (~$10^8–$10^9 per frontier model) is paid by the provider and amortised into per-token API pricing; the deployer pays orchestration ($500–$50K) plus per-token API fees, totalling the $500 / $50K tier figures. *Open-source frontier regime* (Kimi K2.6 from Moonshot, GLM-5.x from Zhipu AI, DeepSeek V4, Qwen 3.6 from Alibaba, Llama 4 from Meta, and adjacent open-weight frontier releases): pre-training cost is paid by the provider and the weights are released free for downstream use; the deployer pays only *compute-of-inference* (running the model on commodity hardware or rented GPU capacity). In this regime, the $500 / $50K tiers are honest *total* cost — orchestration + inference compute, with pre-training a free externalised sunk cost the deployer does not pay. Multiple frontier open-source models are available concurrently as of paper publication; the open-source frontier regime is operational, not aspirational.

It does not guarantee the aims above are realised — adoption, ecosystem, and enclosure dynamics remain open. It removes the substrate-level obstacle that forty years of attempts at composable infrastructure kept hitting. Whether the composability dividend actually lands depends on adoption uptake, on preservation of openness (the AGPL licensing of the substrate layer is the structural preemption against enclosure), and on whether the clients built on the substrate actually compose in practice. None of these is a claim the paper proves; all are conditional on the substrate being operational, which it is.

The honest framing: the paper demonstrates that the substrate forty years of prior waves needed has been built and is open. What that enables downstream is a matter for adoption and time, not theorem.

## Appendix B. The gradient-bound argument against transformer-scaling-as-alternative-path (extended development)

§13's positive case argues that recursion under A + C + D on the typed-graph substrate is architecture-sufficient for AGI. This appendix carries the *bilateral* negative case: the dominant alternative path — transformer-scaling-alone — is mechanically bounded by the architecture's fixed inductive biases, regardless of compute and data. The argument is calibrated to a specific industry assumption (transformer scaling alone reaches AGI) and formalises the structured-AI critique that LeCun, Bengio, and Chollet have articulated for several years.

**The mechanical claim.** Within the current transformer architectural family — autoregressive next-token prediction over fixed token vocabulary with attention-and-feedforward layers — gradient descent on any loss function with any training data reaches representations bounded by the architecture's inductive biases. The architecture's representable function space is a property of attention shape, residual-stream structure, position encoding, layer normalisation, embedding dimensionality, and the autoregressive next-token objective; compute and data do not modify these. If the AGI-required concept space S₂ requires representations the architecture's biases cannot encode, no quantity of training data and no quantity of compute, applied via gradient descent on the current architectural family, constructs S₂ from the trained S₁. The argument is mechanical: gradient descent is bounded by the architecture's representable function space; the architecture is fixed; therefore the reachable representations are fixed; therefore concepts outside the reachable set are unreachable from scaling alone. This formalises the structured-AI critique articulated by LeCun (2022), Bengio et al. (2023), and Chollet (2019), each of whom has argued that autoregressive next-token prediction on statistical substrates is architecturally insufficient for intelligence and that what is needed is not currently supplied by scaling.

**Composition with Theorem XIII (bilateral structure).** The gradient-bound argument composes with Theorem XIII (Paper I §9.4) at complementary rigour levels: Theorem XIII gives the *quantitative within-substrate* sample-complexity bound (kernel learning is exponential in the substrate-richness gap); the gradient-bound argument gives the *qualitative across-substrate* architectural-impossibility bound (scaling cannot reach representations the architecture's biases foreclose). Together they form the bilateral negative case: scaling within the current family is bounded by the family's inductive biases, and scaling within a richness-deficient substrate is bounded exponentially in sample complexity, regardless of compute and data. The framework's positive case (§13) — recursion under A + C + D on the typed-graph substrate — is one specific architecture with substantially different inductive biases (typed-edge composition, reflective-substrate access, weight-function modulation over a graph rather than over a token sequence). Whether it is sufficient for AGI is the empirical asymptote question developed in §13; that the dominant alternative path's bound exists is mechanical.

**Scope of the gradient-bound argument.** A standard objection: the same "fixed inductive biases" argument applies to any architecture, including graph-native — gradient descent on any specific architecture is bounded by that architecture's biases, so the framework is not exempt. Correct. The gradient-bound argument is *not* "transformer architectures cannot reach AGI"; it is "gradient descent on any specific fixed architectural family is bounded by that family's representable function space." Architectural innovation IS the path the framework's methodology calls for, and graph-native is one such architectural innovation that the framework predicts has materially different (and more permissive for AGI-relevant operations) inductive biases. Other structured-AI paths exist and are consistent with the framework's methodology rather than competing with it: mixture-of-experts (MoE), retrieval augmentation, tool use, multi-modal training, neurosymbolic integration, structured state spaces, world-model approaches (LeCun's research programme), and structured-cognition variants are all instances of "moving off the fixed transformer architecture in directions that change the inductive biases." Several of these are converging toward graph-native primitives (typed-edge composition, structured retrieval, world-model substrates) without naming the convergence; the framework's claim is that the typed-graph substrate is one specific articulation of where multiple structured-AI paths converge, not that it is the only path.

**The bilateral structure is intellectual honesty.** The framework names what its substrate offers (positive case, §13) and what the dominant alternative path's architectural bound forecloses (this appendix). Readers committed to the alternative path are invited to engage with the gradient-bound argument directly rather than treat scaling-reaches-AGI as an unnamed assumption. The bilateral case is calibrated to the *dominant industry assumption* (transformer scaling alone reaches AGI), which is what the gradient-bound argument falsifies; alternative structured-AI paths are correctly seen as adjacent rather than competing.

### AI forecasting and horizon evidence (Section 9 references — horizon compression)

- Grace, K., Stewart, H., Sandkühler, J. F., Thomas, S., Weinstein-Raun, B., and Brauner, J. (2024). "Thousands of AI Authors on the Future of AI." AI Impacts and University of Oxford. arXiv:2401.02843. Survey of 2,778 researchers who published at top-tier AI venues (NeurIPS, ICML, ICLR, AAAI, IJCAI, JMLR). Key finding: median expert forecast for "high-level machine intelligence" shifted 13 years earlier between 2022 and 2023 surveys.
- Kurzweil, R. (2005). *The Singularity Is Near: When Humans Transcend Biology*. Viking. Dates human-level AI at 2029 and a full singularity at 2045; reaffirmed in Kurzweil, R. (2024) *The Singularity Is Nearer*, Viking.
- Metaculus (2026). *Aggregated AGI probability forecasts*. `metaculus.com` community aggregation; 25% by 2029, 50% by 2033 as of February 2026. Retrieved 2026-04-24.
- Epoch AI (2024–2026). *Notable AI Models database*. Published under CC-BY-4.0 at `epoch.ai/data/notable-ai-models`. 1,004 models with publication dates 1950–2026 (accessed 2026-04-24); training compute, parameters, organisation, and accessibility metadata per entry.
- METR (2026). *Time Horizon 1.1: an updated measurement of AI's autonomy time-horizons*. `metr.org/blog/2026-1-29-time-horizon-1-1/`, 2026-01-29. Documents agentic-task time-horizon P50 doubling at 196.5 days full-period and **130.8 days post-2023 (≈ 4.3 mo)**; Apr 2026 frontier 50%-success time-horizons: Opus 4.5 = 320 min, GPT-5 = 214 min, o3 = 121 min.
- LessWrong contributor (2026). "METR Time Horizons: Now 10×/Year." `lesswrong.com/posts/EYb2K9acKfyG2bome/metr-time-horizons-now-10x-year`, 2026-02-13. Re-fits METR TH1.1 data on a 2025-only window to give P50 doubling ≈ 3 months (≈ 10×/year), with 2024–2025 at 4-month doubling. Independent corroboration of the post-2023 capability-task acceleration.
- AI Futures Project (2026). *Q1 2026 Timelines Update*. `blog.aifutures.org/p/q1-2026-timelines-update`. Daniel Kokotajlo and Eli Lifland cut median capability-doubling estimate from 5.5 mo → 4–4.5 mo, citing Opus 4.6 + Gemini 3 + GPT-5.2 + METR TH1.1; Daniel cuts his 80%-horizon-for-AC requirement from 3 yr → 1 yr; AC median moves from late-2029 → mid-2028.
- Epoch AI (2025–2026). *AI capabilities progress has sped up*. `epoch.ai/data-insights/ai-capabilities-progress-has-sped-up/`. Documents an inflection in the Epoch Capabilities Index at April 2024: ≈ 8 pts/year before, ≈ 15 pts/year after — independently corroborated in `epoch.ai/blog/have-ai-capabilities-accelerated`. Inflection has not reversed in the 24 months since.
- Hjorth-Jensen, A., et al. (2025). "Forecasting AI Time Horizon Under Compute Slowdowns." arXiv:2511.19492. Derives time-horizon growth proportional to compute under a Cobb-Douglas-style model; propagates uncertainty under slowdown scenarios. Conceptually adjacent to §9 Evidence 3 but does not collapse to a Lyapunov bound; the σ₀-and-rate construction in this paper is original to AI capability forecasting, with Lorenz 1963 (atmospheric predictability) as the canonical chaotic-systems precedent.
- Lorenz, E. N. (1963). "Deterministic Nonperiodic Flow." *Journal of the Atmospheric Sciences*, 20(2): 130–141. Foundational treatment of forecast-horizon collapse under exponential divergence in chaotic systems; the conceptual precedent for §9 Evidence 3's Lyapunov-style horizon bound applied to AI capability.

### Compute scaling, labour economics, and historical adoption (Sections 8, 9, 10, 13 references — dual inflection, horizon, zero-cost, AGI as emergent recursion)

- Acemoglu, D. (2024). "The Simple Macroeconomics of AI." NBER Working Paper 32487. Projects a modest aggregate effect of AI on US GDP (1.1–1.6% cumulative over 10 years). Provides the conservative-end bound of current economic forecasts.
- Acemoglu, D. and Restrepo, P. (2019). "Automation and New Tasks: How Technology Displaces and Reinstates Labor." *Journal of Economic Perspectives*, 33(2): 3–30. Canonical task-based framework for analysing automation's effect on labour. The tasks-vs-roles distinction §10 builds on is their displacement-vs-reinstatement effect, with a mechanical bound added.
- Autor, D. (2015). "Why Are There Still So Many Jobs? The History and Future of Workplace Automation." *Journal of Economic Perspectives*, 29(3): 3–30. Task-based view of labour markets; job-polarisation evidence consistent with routine-cognitive-task automation first.
- Brynjolfsson, E. (1993). "The Productivity Paradox of Information Technology." *Communications of the ACM*, 36(12): 66–77. Documented a 20-year lag between IT capability and IT productivity in the 1970s–80s. *"The bottleneck was never the technology but the organisational restructuring required to use it well."* Historical analog for §4 and §10's coordination-layer argument: the substrate is the organisational restructuring, made mechanical.
- Kaplan, J., McCandlish, S., Henighan, T., Brown, T. B., Chess, B., Child, R., Gray, S., Radford, A., Wu, J., and Amodei, D. (2020). "Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models." arXiv:2001.08361. Empirical power-law relationships between model loss and compute, parameters, and data — the capability-curve underpinning §8.
- Moore, G. E. (1965). "Cramming More Components onto Integrated Circuits." *Electronics*, 38(8): 114–117. The canonical compute-scaling prediction; held for 50+ years before AI-era scaling overtook it by ~100×.
- Sevilla, J., Heim, L., Ho, A., Besiroglu, T., Hobbhahn, M., and Villalobos, P. (2022). "Compute Trends Across Three Eras of Machine Learning." arXiv:2202.05924. Documents AI training compute scaling ~300,000× from 2012 to 2022, with a sharp trend break around 2010–2012. Source for Figure 1's envelope fit.

### General Purpose Technology and cost-decline laws (Section 8 references — dual inflection prior art)

- Bresnahan, T. F. and Trajtenberg, M. (1995). "General Purpose Technologies: 'Engines of Growth'?" *Journal of Econometrics*, 65(1): 83–108. Foundational treatment of general-purpose technologies as substrate transitions producing cost-decline-and-capability-rise dynamics across multiple downstream domains.
- Farmer, J. D. and Lafond, F. (2016). "How Predictable is Technological Progress?" *Research Policy*, 45(3): 647–665. Systematic analysis of cost-decline trajectories across 53 technologies; extends Wright's Law empirically.
- Lipsey, R. G., Carlaw, K. I., and Bekar, C. T. (2005). *Economic Transformations: General Purpose Technologies and Long-Term Economic Growth*. Oxford University Press. Multi-millennium catalogue of substrate transitions with cost-decline-and-capability-rise dynamics.
- Nordhaus, W. D. (1996). "Do Real-Output and Real-Wage Measures Capture Reality? The History of Lighting Suggests Not." In T. F. Bresnahan and R. J. Gordon (eds.), *The Economics of New Goods*, 27–70. University of Chicago Press. Canonical multi-century cost-decline measurement: the price of light from open fires through electric LEDs, falling by orders of magnitude across substrate transitions.
- Wright, T. P. (1936). "Factors Affecting the Cost of Airplanes." *Journal of the Aeronautical Sciences*, 3(4): 122–128. Original formulation of the cumulative-production cost-decline law subsequently named Wright's Law.

### Bounds, information-theoretic limits, and collapse dynamics (Section 13 references — asymptote prior art)

- Bekenstein, J. D. (1981). "Universal Upper Bound on the Entropy-to-Energy Ratio for Bounded Systems." *Physical Review D*, 23(2): 287–298. The Bekenstein bound on information density per unit volume; physical limit on any computational substrate.
- Diamond, J. (2005). *Collapse: How Societies Choose to Fail or Succeed*. Viking. Comparative case studies of civilisational substrate-exhaustion and adaptation outcomes.
- Landauer, R. (1961). "Irreversibility and Heat Generation in the Computing Process." *IBM Journal of Research and Development*, 5(3): 183–191. The kT ln 2 thermodynamic floor on information operations; physical limit on any substrate's energy efficiency.
- Lloyd, S. (2006). *Programming the Universe: A Quantum Computer Scientist Takes on the Cosmos*. Knopf. Universal computational bounds (~10^120 ops in the observable universe); cosmological-scale limits on any substrate.
- Meadows, D. H., Meadows, D. L., Randers, J., and Behrens, W. W. (1972). *The Limits to Growth*. Universe Books. Foundational systems-dynamics treatment of substrate-exhaustion bounds at civilisational scope.
- Smil, V. (2017). *Energy and Civilization: A History*. MIT Press. Multi-millennium catalogue of energy substrates and their scaling limits across civilisations.
- Tainter, J. A. (1988). *The Collapse of Complex Societies*. Cambridge University Press. Foundational treatment of complexity-cost dynamics and substrate-exhaustion as causes of civilisational asymptotes; scale-invariant in form.

### Affective computing, biochemistry, and attention-mechanism prior art (Section 12 references — biochemical/physiological modality and formal-attention sharpening)

- Aston-Jones, G. and Cohen, J. D. (2005). "An Integrative Theory of Locus Coeruleus–Norepinephrine Function: Adaptive Gain and Optimal Performance." *Annual Review of Neuroscience*, 28: 403–450. Neuromodulatory framework relating norepinephrine to attentional gain and exploration-exploitation balance.
- Damasio, A. R. (1994). *Descartes' Error: Emotion, Reason, and the Human Brain*. Putnam. Somatic marker hypothesis: emotion as integral to cognition rather than separable from it; mechanism for affect-as-weighting on decision substrate.
- James, W. (1890). *The Principles of Psychology*. Henry Holt. Foundational articulation of attention-as-an-operation in cognitive psychology — the conceptual antecedent that transformer attention (Vaswani et al. 2017) supplies the first machine-implementable specification of, ~135 years later.
- Picard, R. W. (1997). *Affective Computing*. MIT Press. Foundational framework for machines that recognise, interpret, and simulate human affect; the research programme §12's biochemical/physiological modality lineage extends.
- Russell, J. A. (1980). "A Circumplex Model of Affect." *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*, 39(6): 1161–1178. Two-dimensional (valence × arousal) model of affect; the canonical structured representation §12 references for affective state.
- Sapolsky, R. M. (2004). *Why Zebras Don't Get Ulcers* (3rd edition). W. H. Freeman. (First edition 1994.) Cortisol and chronic-stress neuroendocrinology; the biochemical mechanism producing time-varying attentional and cognitive weight distributions.
- Schultz, W., Dayan, P., and Montague, P. R. (1997). "A Neural Substrate of Prediction and Reward." *Science*, 275(5306): 1593–1599. Dopamine as reward-prediction-error signal; the foundational neuroscience for reinforcement-learning-based weight modulation.
- Vaswani, A., Shazeer, N., Parmar, N., Uszkoreit, J., Jones, L., Gomez, A. N., Kaiser, Ł., and Polosukhin, I. (2017). "Attention Is All You Need." *NeurIPS 2017*. arXiv:1706.03762. The first machine-implementable specification of attention-as-an-operation (`softmax(QK^T/√d)V`); closes the ~135-year gap to William James's 1890 conceptual articulation.
